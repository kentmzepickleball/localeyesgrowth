Every catering event carries roughly $300 to $500 of cost before you hand anyone a drink. That number does not care whether you serve 30 guests or 130, and it is the reason most catering quotes are built backwards.

If you want one rule for how to price catering, it is this: build the number up from your true cost, then divide by your target margin. Do not mark up your ingredients. Below is the full cost stack, the formula, and a worked 100-guest quote that lands within a few dollars of the national median for exactly that job.

## Why cost-of-goods pricing destroys catering margins

Restaurant pricing runs on food cost. A cafe targets around 30 percent cost of goods, prices at roughly three times ingredients, and that works because the product is the cup.

Catering does not work that way. A 100-guest, two-hour coffee service is about 50 to 60 drinks and somewhere between $50 and $75 of actual product. Price that on a 33 percent cost-of-goods model and you would quote around $170 for a day that costs you eight hours and a vehicle. Mobile carts run cost of goods near 10 percent, versus about 30 percent for a cafe, which is precisely why the percentage model breaks.

Your product is not the coffee or the cocktail. It is labor, logistics, and the promise that you show up on a specific date and do not embarrass the person who hired you. Price the job, not the ingredients. The deeper version of this mistake is covered in pricing out of your own wallet.

## The catering cost stack

Five buckets, in the order most operators forget them.

| Cost bucket | What it covers | Typical range |
|---|---|---|
| Off-event labor | Shop, prep, pack, drive, set up, tear down, return, clean, restock | 6 to 9 hours per event |
| Consumables | Product, cups, garnish, napkins, ice | $0.50 to $0.60 per guest |
| Travel | Free radius, commonly 30 miles, then per-mile beyond it | $2 to $3 per mile past the radius |
| Insurance | General liability, plus liquor liability for bar work | $60 to $100 per event |
| Owner time | Sales, admin, quoting, invoicing, follow-up | Priced at what you would pay a replacement |

That last row is the one that sinks people. The most-forgotten piece in catering pricing is the owner's own labor. If you do not pay yourself for the eight hours, you never generate the money to hire someone else to do them, and your business has a hard ceiling at your personal stamina. Operators who eventually scaled almost all describe paying themselves something per event early on, even a token $75, purely to force the number into the math.

## The formula

Here is the part most operators get wrong, and it costs real money.

```
Price = Total cost / (1 - target margin)
```

Not cost plus a percentage. Adding 50 percent to your cost does not give you a 50 percent margin, it gives you 33 percent. The two are different numbers and the gap widens as your target rises.

| Target margin | Divide cost by | Effective multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| 50% | 0.50 | 2.0x |
| 60% | 0.40 | 2.5x |
| 65% | 0.35 | 2.9x |
| 70% | 0.30 | 3.3x |

Anything above 50 percent gross is considered healthy across the verticals. Mobile bars generally target 50 to 60 percent. Coffee catering runs higher on gross, commonly 85 to 90 percent, settling to 60 to 70 percent net once you account for everything. Once a business adds a warehouse, admin staff, and vehicles, net compresses to the 15 to 25 percent band.

## A worked quote: 100 guests, two hours, coffee service

Real numbers, built from the stack above.

| Line | Calculation | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Off-event labor | 8 hours at $30/hr (shop 2, pack 1, drive and set up 2, break down and return 2, clean 1) | $240 |
| Consumables | 100 guests at $0.55 | $55 |
| Travel | 30-mile round trip, baked into base | $0 |
| Insurance | Per-event allocation | $60 |
| **Total cost** | | **$355** |
| **Price at 65% margin** | $355 / 0.35 | **$1,014** |

Now the sanity check. The Flashquotes Coffee Catering Pricing Dataset (2023-2026), covering 25,860 bookings across 578 operators and released under CC BY 4.0, puts the median coffee catering booking at roughly $1,040, with the middle half falling between $765 and $1,700.

A cost stack built honestly from the bottom lands within about $26 of what the market actually pays for that job. That convergence is the whole argument. The formula is not a pricing philosophy, it is a way of arriving at the number the market already supports instead of guessing low.

Compare that with the cost-of-goods approach: $55 of product at a 33 percent model gives you $167. You would work a nine-hour day for less than minimum wage and call it a booking.

## What moves the number up

The cost stack gives you the floor. These are the levers that lift the actual quote:

- **Duration.** The standard structure is a base package covering the first two hours, then an hourly rate on top, commonly $150 to $200 per additional service hour. Two hours is both the default window for more than half of single-day bookings and the usual billing minimum, because load-in, dial-in, and teardown cost the same whether you pour for one hour or four.
- **Guest count, but as a scale effect.** Per-guest cost drops roughly 75 percent as events scale, from around $38 per guest under 50 people to around $10 per guest at 500-plus. Your per-head rate should step down as counts rise, because your fixed costs spread.
- **Add-ons with high perceived value and low cost.** Custom cart branding, logo cups, a premium menu, latte-art printing. These lift the total without touching your base rate.
- **Throughput, not headcount.** One barista comfortably sustains 45 to 60 drinks an hour. A 100-guest, 3-hour event is roughly 150 drinks, about 50 an hour, which one skilled barista handles. Compress the same event into 2 hours and you need a second. Staff to throughput, then price the staff.
- **Positioning.** Average booking value is the highest-leverage lever in the entire business. The same $100,000 year is 200 events at $500, roughly four a week and brutal, or 50 events at $2,000, roughly one a week. Same revenue, completely different life.

## How to know your price is wrong

Your booking rate is the scoreboard, and it reads in both directions. It is one of several checks in how to price a mobile catering business.

- **Below 15 percent.** Either the price is genuinely too high for what you are communicating, or, far more often, the problem is upstream in speed and presentation rather than the number itself. Quote speed alone swings booking rates by roughly 10 points, which is covered in [the 5-minute rule](/blog/5-minute-rule-quote-speed).
- **15 to 30 percent.** Healthy. This is the band to hold.
- **Above 30 percent, or anything near 100 percent.** You are underpriced. A booking rate that high means you are the cheap option, or you are not getting enough leads to be selective.

Two field tests operators use to find the ceiling: raise your baseline by $25 to $50 every five events and read the reaction, or raise after every three bookings that closed with no pushback and immediate payment. A quote that gets accepted instantly and without a single question was too low. And when a happy client rebooks and asks you to do more, that is the market telling you what you could have charged.

If a price feels too low but you are afraid to move it, the honest diagnosis is usually not pricing at all. It is that not enough qualified people are finding you, so every quote feels like one you cannot afford to lose. Fixing demand is what makes raising prices survivable.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much should I charge for catering?

For coffee catering, the national median booking is around $1,040, with the middle half of bookings between $765 and $1,700 (Flashquotes, CC BY 4.0). Mobile bar operators commonly set a minimum around $1,750, with a 150-guest dry hire landing between $1,750 and $2,500. For a 100-guest, 3-hour espresso cart event the market median benchmarks around $1,105, with value operators near $880 and premium carts near $1,480. Use those as sanity checks, not as your price. Build your own number from your cost stack, then compare it against our [coffee catering price calculator](/coffee-cart-pricing-calculator).

### What is a good profit margin for a catering business?

Above 50 percent gross is the general benchmark. Mobile bars typically target 50 to 60 percent. Coffee catering runs 85 to 90 percent gross and 60 to 70 percent net for a lean operation, compressing toward 15 to 25 percent net once you add warehouse space, admin staff, and multiple vehicles.

### Should I price per person, per hour, or as a flat package?

It depends on what actually constrains you. Coffee service is effectively unlimited during the window, so it prices by the hour. Bar work with distinct drinks per guest supports per-person pricing. Most operators sell flat tiered packages because they are easier to buy, and calculate per-unit underneath so the package stays profitable regardless of what the client picks. The full comparison is in per-person vs flat vs hourly pricing.

### Do I include setup and teardown in the price?

Always, one way or the other. Either bake it into the base rate or bill it as a line item, but never absorb it. Setup and teardown are commonly two to three hours on each side, which is often more clock time than the service itself. Charcuterie operators frequently bill setup at around $75 and setup plus breakdown at around $100 as explicit line items. Travel works the same way: a free radius of around 30 miles, then $2 to $3 per mile beyond it. Fold travel into the base and you quietly discount every distant event.

### How often should I raise my prices?

Raise by $25 to $50 every five events, or after any three consecutive bookings that closed with no negotiation and immediate payment. Both approaches use the market to find your ceiling rather than guessing at it. Ease back if you hit real resistance, and hold the new number if you do not.
