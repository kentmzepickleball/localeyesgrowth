Google sandboxes brand new domains for roughly five to seven weeks before they start ranking. That single fact should reorder your entire startup checklist: build the website and start SEO before you buy the cart or rig, not after, so the domain is already aging by the time you are ready to take your first booking.

Most new operators do the opposite. They spend weeks agonizing over equipment, launch the business, and only then remember they need a website, by which point they have burned the exact runway that could have been ranking in the background. Here is the sequence that avoids that, whether you are starting a coffee cart, a mobile bar, or a charcuterie business.

## The 6-step startup sequence

Work through these in order. Skipping ahead to equipment is the single most common mistake operators report making.

| Step | What to do | Why order matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Decide your end goal | Side hustle, full-time income, or a stepping stone to something bigger | Every downstream decision, entity type, how scrappy to start, how fast to invest, depends on this answer |
| 2. Practice for free | Run a handful of events for friends and family before spending money or forming an entity | You build a portfolio and confirm you actually want to do this, and staying unpaid keeps it legal before licensing is in place |
| 3. Cover yourself legally | Form an entity (almost always an LLC), get the right local permits, buy insurance, get certified if required, have a contract template ready | This has to happen before you take paid bookings, and rules vary by state, county, and sometimes city, so start early |
| 4. Build the website and start SEO | Launch a real site with a clear service and location, before the cart exists | New domains take weeks to months to start ranking, so this step should run in parallel with equipment, not after it |
| 5. Buy equipment, scrappy first | A folding table, a repurposed desk, a low-cost cart, whatever gets you operating without overextending | Operators who launched on a few hundred dollars of equipment booked real events within weeks. The rig is not the business |
| 6. Launch and book work | Take your first paid events, start collecting reviews and photos, reinvest into better equipment as revenue allows | This is where most operators start, which is why steps 1 through 5 are usually skipped or rushed |

## Cover yourself legally, in the right order

Step 3 is where most of the real variation by vertical shows up, and it is worth breaking down because "get legal" is not one task.

**Entity first.** An LLC is the near-universal recommendation across every vertical, because it puts a liability barrier between the business and your personal assets. Charcuterie operators sometimes register a DBA first so they can collect money under a business name before the full entity is in place, then form the LLC.

**Permits second, and they vary by location more than any other part of this list.** A mobile bar in one state may operate under a straightforward dry-hire model with minimal permitting, while a neighboring state requires a specific mobile food facility license even for alcohol-adjacent service. Charcuterie needs a health permit, which usually requires a commercial kitchen relationship already in place, or a cottage food license to prep from home where that is allowed. Call your local health department and business licensing office directly. Do not rely on another operator's setup, since what applied in their county may not apply in yours.

**Insurance third, and treat it as non-negotiable.** General liability at minimum, liquor liability if alcohol is involved in any way (even dry hire), and commercial auto if you are towing. The cost to exit a lawsuit even when you are not found liable commonly runs tens of thousands of dollars, which is the real argument for never going without coverage, not the small chance of losing a specific case.

**Certification and contract last.** A responsible-service certification if you are anywhere near alcohol, a food-handler certification for charcuterie, and a contract template reviewed by an attorney rather than copied from another operator's business. Contract details differ by state and by what you are actually promising to deliver.

## Build the website before you buy the cart

This is the step most startup guides skip entirely, and it is the one with the clearest evidence behind it. New domains sit in a Google sandbox for weeks to months before ranking activity begins in earnest. If you wait until you have the cart, the insurance, and the first booking to think about a website, you are starting that clock months later than you needed to.

![Website timeline versus cart timeline when starting a mobile catering business](/site-vs-cart-timeline.svg)

The website at this stage does not need to be elaborate. It needs a clear statement of what you do and where you serve, a way for people to contact you, and enough real content that Google has something to index while the domain ages. Photos and reviews can come later. Existing is what starts the clock.

## Start scrappy, then reinvest

Every vertical has examples of operators who started on a few hundred dollars of equipment and booked meaningful early revenue. A folding table and a handful of business cards. A repurposed desk bought secondhand. A low-cost cart found on a local marketplace. None of these are the finished version of the business, and none of them stopped the operator from getting booked.

The instinct to buy the polished version of the rig before taking a single booking is the most common form of startup paralysis in this industry. Pull the trigger on the best option available now, book your first events on it, and reinvest as revenue allows. A nicer setup funded by real bookings is a better position than a perfect setup funded by savings and zero proof the business works.

## FAQ

### What should I do first when starting a mobile catering business?
Decide your end goal before anything else, since it changes every downstream decision. Then practice for free on friends and family to confirm the model and build a portfolio, get legally covered (entity, permits, insurance, certification), start your website and SEO in parallel with buying equipment, and launch with scrappy gear rather than waiting for the perfect setup.

### Do I need an LLC before I take my first booking?
You should have the entity formed before you take paid work, since that is when liability exposure begins. Practicing for free with friends and family before forming the entity is common and generally fine, because nothing is being sold. Once money changes hands, the LLC and the rest of the legal stack (permits, insurance) need to be in place.

### Should I build my website before I buy my cart or rig?
Yes. New domains take roughly five to seven weeks, sometimes longer, to start ranking in Google, so the site should be live and aging while your equipment is still being sourced. Waiting until the cart arrives to think about a website means losing that entire runway.

### How much does it cost to start a mobile catering business?
It ranges enormously by vertical and how scrappy you start, from a few hundred dollars for a folding-table setup to $20,000 or more for a fully built-out trailer or cart. The full cost breakdown by vertical, coffee cart, mobile bar, and charcuterie, is covered in what it actually costs to start, by vertical.

### Do I need a liquor license to start a mobile bar?
Most mobile bars operate without one, using a dry-hire model where the client purchases the alcohol directly and you provide everything else, staffing, mixers, equipment, and service. The specifics vary by state, so this deserves its own research before you launch. See dry hire explained for the full breakdown.
